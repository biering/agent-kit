# Page formats

A format is the order in which the page earns the click it asks for. Pick it from how much the
reader already knows when they arrive, never from taste. One page, one format.

## How warm is the click?

| Reader arrives… | Knows the problem | Knows a solution exists | Knows us | Format |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold: interrupted in a feed (Meta, TikTok, LinkedIn) | feels it, has no name for it | no | no | **Gap** or **Advertorial** |
| Lukewarm: clicked a "how do I…" or a pain ad | yes | vaguely | no | **Gap** or **Before/After** |
| Comparing: searched a category or a competitor (Google) | yes | yes | maybe | **Listicle** or **Before/After** |
| Warm: retargeting, newsletter, brand search | yes | yes | yes | **Demo-first** |

When two fit, take the colder one. A page written for a cold reader still works on a warm one;
the reverse loses the cold reader in the first screen.

## 1. Gap

Names a disconnect the reader lives with and has stopped noticing, then closes it.

1. Hero: the gap as one sentence, the product as the last two words.
2. Proof strip: what it works with.
3. Demo: the product answering the reader's own question.
4. The gap, spelled out: two cards, each one thing that does not connect.
5. Before/After with time per step.
6. How it works: three steps with a timestamp each.
7. Closing CTA that repeats the gap.

Use when the ad's hook is a tension ("Your listing does not know your bank's rate").

**Fails when** the gap is one the reader does not feel. Test: say it to a person from the
audience. You should get "yes, exactly" back with no explanation in between.

## 2. Before/After

The whole page is one comparison: the way the reader does it today against the way it goes with
the product.

1. Hero: the outcome with its number ("Analysis in 2 minutes instead of an evening").
2. The two columns, step by step, with time or effort per row.
3. The one mechanism that makes the right column possible.
4. Demo or screenshot of the result.
5. Objections (three, each answered in one sentence).
6. Closing CTA.

Use when the reader has a routine they know is slow: a spreadsheet, a folder of PDFs, a chat with
a general-purpose assistant. The left column is the reader's real routine, row for row. A strawman
left column ("hours of despair") is read as an ad and skipped.

## 3. Advertorial (story-led)

Reads like an article by someone who had the problem. For the coldest traffic and the most
sceptical reader. It educates before it names the product.

1. Headline in the voice of an article: a finding, a mistake, a number.
2. A specific opening scene: one person, one object, one moment the problem cost them.
3. Why the usual way fails (the mechanism of the problem, in plain words).
4. The turn: what changes it. The product enters here, in the second half.
5. What it looks like in use: one worked case with real numbers.
6. Proof, the offer, the CTA. Then the CTA once more after a short recap.

Rules that keep it honest. It is labelled as ours: the logo in the header and the ad label where
a platform requires it. The person in the story is real and agreed to it, or the case is
presented as a worked calculation, never as a customer. No invented quotes, ever.

**Longer than the others on purpose.** 600 to 1,000 words, short paragraphs, a subheading every
screen, a CTA after the turn and at the end.

## 4. Listicle (scannable, numbered)

For the reader who is comparing and reads in fragments.

1. Headline with the number and the outcome ("7 things the listing leaves out and the data room
   holds").
2. One line of why this list.
3. The items: number, a bold claim of at most eight words, two sentences, optionally a small
   visual. Each item stands alone.
4. The product is the mechanism in several items, and the answer in the last one.
5. CTA after item 3 (soft, text link) and after the last item (button).

Five to nine items. An odd number reads less manufactured than ten. Every item is one the reader
can check: a document, a clause, a figure. An item that is a product feature in disguise ("7. Our
great interface") spends the trust the other six built.

## 5. Demo-first

For the reader who already wants it and needs to see it once.

1. Hero is the product doing the job: an input the reader can use on the page, or a recorded
   result with real numbers.
2. Three facts under it: what goes in, what comes out, what it costs.
3. Objections.
4. CTA.

Short. A page that needs a paragraph to explain why anyone would want this got a colder click
than you thought: use another format.

## What every format shares

- **One action.** The same CTA wording in every position.
- **No way off the page** except the CTA, the legal links and, where the brand's layout has one,
  the login link. No main nav, no blog teaser, no "more products".
- **The first screen holds the promise of the ad**, the CTA and one reason to believe it, on a
  390 px wide phone, with no scrolling.
- **Problem before product.** The product name is never the first noun on the page.
- **The offer lowers the risk** in the line next to the button: what the first step costs, and
  what it does not require.
- **A sticky CTA on mobile** once the hero CTA has scrolled out of view.
